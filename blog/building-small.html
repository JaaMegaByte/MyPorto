Building Small: Fewer Moving Parts, Easier to Trust

Feb 21, 2026 Reza Dwi Putra 4 min read

Most of what I build is small on purpose. Not because small is a virtue in itself, but because a smaller system is one I can actually hold in my head: every dependency, every failure mode, every place state can get out of sync. That's a real advantage, and it's an easy one to trade away for convenience.

Every dependency is a decision, not a default

It's simple to add a library for something a project could do in twenty lines itself. Sometimes that's the right call. No one should hand-roll a cryptography library. But each dependency is also a piece of behavior you didn't write and don't fully control, with its own bugs, its own breaking changes, and its own maintenance timeline that doesn't match yours. Treating "add a dependency" as a decision with a real cost, rather than a free convenience, tends to keep a project smaller and more predictable over time.

Debugging is proportional to surface area

When something breaks in a small system, there are fewer places it could be. That sounds obvious, but it compounds: a system with five moving parts has roughly five places to check, while a system with fifty has a search space an order of magnitude larger, and the interactions between those parts multiply faster than the parts themselves. A lot of "this bug took three days to find" stories are really stories about surface area that grew faster than anyone was tracking it.

Small is also more honest about scope

A small tool tends to say clearly what it does and doesn't do. A large one accumulates configuration options and edge-case handling until its actual scope is hard to state in a sentence. When I'm deciding how to build something, I try to write down what it needs to do in one sentence first, and treat anything that doesn't fit that sentence as a feature for a different tool, not a reason to expand this one.

None of this rules out building bigger systems. Some problems genuinely need them. But defaulting to small, and only growing a system when there's a concrete reason to, tends to produce software I trust more and enjoy maintaining longer.
